A weathered sandstone hoodoo, a slender spire of layered sedimentary rock that tapers and steps upward from a broad, blocky base to a dramatically overhanging capstone. The overall silhouette reads like an irregular, lopsided pagoda or a melting candle: wide and heavy at the foot, narrowing through a sequence of stacked, projecting ledges, then crowned by a flat, mushroom-like slab that flares out well beyond the neck supporting it. The base is a chunky, rounded mass riddled with shallow hollows and one or two small wind-cut alcoves and arches near the ground, where softer material has eroded away faster than the harder strata above. From there the body climbs as a stack of horizontal beds, each layer differentially weathered so that resistant bands jut out as thin shelves and lips while recessive bands pinch inward, giving the column a ribbed, ratcheting, almost vertebral profile. The dominant and essentially only surface material is bare, fine-grained sandstone, its bedding planes clearly visible as stratified laminations running across the whole form. Color ranges across warm desert tones -- rusty terracotta, burnt orange, ochre and salmon -- mottled with paler buff and tan streaks where mineral staining and sun-bleaching vary the patina. The texture is gritty and granular, rough to rough-smooth, scalloped by wind and water into rounded edges, flaking ledges, and pitted faces rather than sharp crystalline fractures. This kind of feature belongs to arid badlands and high desert plateaus -- canyon country, eroded escarpments and dry washes where exposed sandstone is sculpted by wind, occasional flash flooding, and freeze-thaw into balanced, top-heavy pinnacles.
