A single angular hand-specimen of pale blue crystalline mineral, the kind of fractured rock chunk a collector keeps on a shelf or that a geologist breaks loose from a vein. The overall silhouette is a roughly triangular, wedge-like mass, broadest and tallest toward the left where a blunt peak rises, then stepping and sloping down to a thinner tapered edge on the right; it sits with a flattish irregular base so it rests stably. Structurally the body reads as a stack of cleaved, blocky plates and ledges layered diagonally across the form, as though the stone has split along parallel planes — sharp shelf-like steps, recessed notches, and a few deep cleavage cracks divide it into stacked facets rather than a smooth boulder skin. The dominant surface material is a glassy-to-waxy crystalline mineral in a soft, frosty sky-blue to powder-blue, slightly translucent so that edges and thin flakes glow a touch lighter; broken faces show a fibrous, almost sugary-granular sparkle where light catches fresh fracture planes. Interspersed are zones of chalky milky-white and faint grey, concentrated along high ridges and broken edges where the crystal is more shattered or weathered, giving a frosted, snow-dusted quality. Textures range from comparatively smooth flat cleavage faces to rough, crumbly, splintered margins and crystalline druzy patches. Color is cool throughout — pale cyan-blue grading into white, with subtle bluish-grey shadowing in the recesses. This kind of object belongs in a mineralogical context: cave seams, limestone veins, and quarry pockets in the wild, or a display case, study desk, or museum drawer when collected.
